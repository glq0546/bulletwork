What is FOREIGN TRANSACTION FEE on my credit card statement?

Last updated: November 20, 2026

What is a foreign transaction fee? It is a 3% charge by your bank for buying from merchants outside your country. It appears as a separate line on your statement, next to the purchase that triggered it.

Typical rate: 3% of the purchase amount on most US cards; 0% on cards marketed as having no foreign transaction fee.

Charged by: the card issuer, not the merchant.

Applies when: the payment is processed outside your country, or settles in a currency other than your account currency.

Statement labels: FOREIGN TRANSACTION FEE, INTL SERVICE FEE, INTERNATIONAL SERVICE FEE, CROSS-BORDER FEE, FOREIGN PURCHASE FEE.

Cost examples: $3.00 on a $100.00 purchase; $0.90 on a $30.00 monthly subscription; $150 on $5,000 of foreign spend in one year.

Merchant code: FOREIGN TRANSACTION FEE is an issuer fee line, not a merchant name.

How to check if you were charged a foreign transaction fee

1. Find the fee line. Search the statement PDF or app for FOREIGN TRANSACTION FEE, INTL SERVICE FEE or CROSS-BORDER FEE. The fee is listed as its own line, separate from the merchant charge it belongs to.
2. Match it to the purchase. The fee is posted beside the transaction that triggered it, usually on the same date or one to two days later. Open the transaction detail and read the merchant's billing country, not the currency shown at checkout.
3. Check the math. Divide the fee by the purchase amount. $3.00 on $100.00 is 3%; $4.50 on $150.00 is also 3%. A lower ratio points to a split transaction, and a higher one usually means a separate currency conversion markup or a different rate for that card tier.
4. Confirm your card's rate. The rate is printed in the cardholder agreement and on the issuer's online fee schedule. Tiers differ: travel cards commonly list 0%, everyday rewards and store cards up to 3%, and foreign ATM withdrawals follow a separate schedule.
5. Call the issuer if the line is unclear. Use the number on the back of the card. Ask for four items: the fee name, the rate, the transaction it was applied to, and the clause in the cardholder agreement that authorizes it.

Fraud check: a 3% fee matches the published rate

A fee equal to 3% of an overseas purchase matches the rate disclosed in the cardholder agreement, so it is a standard charge. Treat the line as unauthorized only when no matching overseas or foreign-processed purchase appears on the statement within 48 hours.

How to avoid a foreign transaction fee

1. Move foreign spend to a 0% card. Chase Sapphire Preferred and Capital One Venture both list a 0% foreign transaction fee in their fee schedules. Keep the 3% card for domestic purchases.
2. Pay in the local currency. When a terminal or checkout offers to charge you in US dollars, that is dynamic currency conversion: the merchant or its processor adds a markup of about 3% to 7% on top of any issuer fee. Choosing EUR, GBP or JPY keeps the conversion with your issuer.
3. Reroute foreign subscriptions. Services billed from outside the US apply the fee to every renewal. On a $15.00 monthly plan the 3% fee is $0.45 per month, or $5.40 per year for that one subscription. PADDLE.NET and CANVA charges are common examples.
4. Set the 0% card as the default for travel. Store it on the airline, hotel and foreign retail accounts you use most, so the 3% fee is not applied to those transactions.
5. Check ATM fees separately. Foreign cash withdrawals usually carry the 3% fee plus the ATM operator's charge of about $3 to $5 per withdrawal. A 0% card does not waive the operator fee, and debit rebates are often capped per month.
6. Verify on the next statement. After switching cards, the fee line should be absent from the next billing cycle. If it still appears, confirm the purchase was processed by the new card.

How to get a refund for a foreign transaction fee

1. Check whether a reversal applies. A 3% fee correctly applied to an overseas purchase is authorized by the cardholder agreement, so issuers rarely refund it. Reversal applies when the purchase was refunded and the fee was not, the fee is duplicated, the transaction was domestic, or the order was canceled before it shipped.
2. Call with the transaction details. Give the date, the purchase amount, the merchant name and the fee amount. State the ground, for example: the $100.00 purchase was refunded but the $3.00 fee remained. Ask for a courtesy credit and record the reference number.
3. Put a billing-error claim in writing. Under the Fair Credit Billing Act, a written dispute must reach the issuer within 60 days of the statement date. Send it to the billing-inquiries address printed on the statement, not the general support email.
4. Escalate if the first answer is no. Ask for a supervisor and refer to the specific fee clause. If the issuer does not resolve the claim, file a complaint with the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB).
5. Cut the cost going forward. Refunds of correctly assessed fees are uncommon. Moving foreign purchases to a 0% card removes 3% from each one, which is $150 on $5,000 of annual foreign spend.

FAQ: foreign transaction fees

Why was I charged a foreign transaction fee when I paid in US dollars?

The receipt currency does not decide the fee. The issuer charges it when the payment is routed through a processor outside your country. A UK or Canadian checkout priced in US dollars still settles through a foreign processor, so the 3% fee applies. Open the transaction detail and check the merchant's billing country rather than the currency shown at checkout.

What is the difference between a foreign transaction fee and a currency conversion fee?

A foreign transaction fee is the issuer's percentage, typically 3%, for processing an overseas payment. A currency conversion fee is the markup a merchant or payment processor adds when it converts the price into your currency at checkout, usually 3% to 7%. Both can apply to one purchase, so the same item can cost 6% to 10% above its listed local price.

Is a 3% foreign transaction fee normal in the United States?

Yes. Most US issuers that charge the fee set it at 3% of the purchase amount, and the rate is disclosed in the cardholder agreement and the online fee schedule. Cards marketed as travel cards, including Chase Sapphire Preferred and Capital One Venture, list 0%. Rates above 3% usually come from a currency conversion markup added by the merchant.

Do debit cards and ATM withdrawals charge foreign transaction fees?

Yes. Debit cards commonly carry a 3% foreign transaction fee plus a flat per-withdrawal charge, and the foreign ATM operator adds its own fee, often $3 to $5. The 3% is calculated on the withdrawal amount. Waiving the card fee does not waive the operator fee, and some issuers cap ATM rebates per month.

Does a foreign transaction fee get refunded when I return the purchase?

Not automatically. Issuers usually reverse the fee when the merchant voids or refunds the full purchase, because the underlying transaction no longer exists. If the refund posts and the 3% fee remains, call the issuer with the purchase date and fee amount and ask for the matching reversal. A written billing-error claim is valid within 60 days of the statement date.

How do I find my card's foreign transaction fee before I travel?

Open the cardholder agreement PDF or the issuer's online fee schedule and search for foreign transaction fee or cross-border fee. The rate is listed with the other transaction fees. You can also call the number on the back of the card and ask for the rate and the transaction types it covers. Check the debit and ATM schedule separately.

Can I get a foreign transaction fee removed by calling my bank?

Only with a specific ground: a refunded or canceled purchase, a duplicate fee, a domestic transaction, or a fee not listed in your agreement. Ask for a courtesy credit and note the reference number. A 3% fee correctly applied to an overseas purchase is disclosed in the cardholder agreement, so issuers rarely reverse it. A 0% card prevents the charge.
